What do we educate for, and how AI-exposed is the job?

Each institution awards degrees in particular fields. Each degree typically leads to particular occupations. And each occupation has an AI exposure, the same one the index uses. This page links the three for every Norwegian institution with at least 50 graduates a year. Pick a field and a level to see national numbers and who educates most. Pick an institution to see its graduates, fields and typical occupations.

Summary for the impatient reader
  • The business schools are on top. NHH, BI and Kristiania send graduates into accounting, finance, marketing and case handling. Those are occupations high on the exposure scale.
  • The professional colleges are at the bottom. Teachers, nurses and care workers have low exposure, and institutions that educate many of them get low means.
  • Field and level explain most of it. The difference between institutions is first of all a difference in what they teach. Within the same field and level, institutions look alike, because the occupational profiles are national.

The exposure measure applies to the whole page. Eloundou et al. (2024) is the default, as on the index page. Mouchel et al. (2026) is an evidence-grounded alternative. Both are scaled 0 to 1, and the quintiles split occupations into five equal groups, Q1 least and Q5 most exposed.

Method: how the numbers are made

The numbers are built in three steps, and each step has its own calculation. First, every education code gets an occupational profile and an exposure. Then the codes are grouped into fields and levels. Finally, the programmes at each institution are weighted together into institution numbers.

Step 1: exposure per education code

The starting point is utdanning.no's register statistics, built on Statistics Norway's registers. For each education code in NUS2000 it gives the number of residents aged 20–70 who hold the code as their highest completed education, split by occupation (STYRK-08) for those employed. Those not employed appear as five status categories (self-employed, in education, unemployed, disability benefit, outside the labour force). Cells with one or two persons are removed. The link is from November 2024.

Every occupation has an AI exposure from 0 to 1, the same one the index uses: Eloundou et al. (2024) or Mouchel et al. (2026), mapped to STYRK-08 via ISCO and SOC. The exposure of an education code is the person-weighted mean of the occupations' exposure over the employed who hold an occupation with a score. The share in a quintile is the share of the same persons working in an occupation in that quintile. The status categories and self-employed without an occupation are outside the calculation. Score coverage is above 98 percent for all relevant educations.

Step 2: field and level

The field is the second digit of the NUS code, Statistics Norway's nine fields of study. The level is the first digit, here grouped into bachelor level (6), master level (7), PhD (8), vocational college (5) and upper secondary (3 and 4). Parts 1 and 4 show national numbers: everyone in the register with an education in the group, wherever they studied. The exposure of a group is person-weighted over the education codes in the group, which is the same as weighting each occupation by how many in the group hold it. The ten most common occupations in part 1 come from the group's pooled occupational distribution.

Field and level at an institution (part 2) is something else. There, the programmes the institution offers in the field and at the level are weighted by graduates in the latest year. The level comes from DBH's level code for the programme, not from the NUS code, and the field from the programme's NUS code. PhDs have no NUS code in DBH and get the national profile of all doctoral educations.

Step 3: institution

Graduate counts come from DBH (HK-dir), table 104 "Graduates" per study programme with NUS code and level code, table 101 "Doctoral degrees" and table 123 "Registered students". Every institution with at least 50 graduates in the latest year is included, 35 in all. Graduates are qualifications awarded, not persons.

Each programme gets the national profile of its NUS code from step 1. If the code has fewer than 100 employed persons with a score in the register, the profile of the four-digit NUS group is used instead. The institution's exposure is the graduate-weighted mean over programmes. The share in a quintile is weighted the same way. The most common occupations for the institution are the graduate-weighted sum of the programmes' occupational distributions.

DBH suppresses small cells, so programme rows sum slightly below the institution's total. The total in the card is DBH's exact figure, while the mix across fields, levels and occupations comes from the programme rows. The share "programme rows with a profile" says how many of the graduates got a profile.

The main simplification: profiles are national per education code, not per institution. A business graduate from BI and one from NHH get the same occupational profile when the programmes share a NUS code. The difference between institutions is therefore a difference in what they teach, not in where their graduates end up.

The quintiles

Occupations with a score are split into five equal groups by exposure, as on the index page. Each occupation counts once. The cut-offs are computed per measure. The share in Q5 is the share of employed persons with a score who work in an occupation in the most exposed fifth.

Caveats
  1. Occupational profiles are national per education code, not per institution.
  2. The register link is from November 2024 and covers residents aged 20 to 70. Graduate counts are for 2025.
  3. Recent graduates rest on a column in utdanning.no that is not documented in the API. We read it as completed one to three years ago.
  4. Both exposure measures are theoretical task assessments, not observed use.
  5. Small institutions get thinner numbers because DBH suppresses small cells. Institutions with fewer than 50 graduates are left out.
